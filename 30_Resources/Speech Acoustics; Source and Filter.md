
Source-filter model: the sound source of the speech is vibration so of the vocal air or turbulent air
- where as the filter is the vocal tract shape determined by placement/shape of the articulators

- tongue position is a huge factor
- as it causes some positions to be amplified and some to be dampened
- the tones that are amplified are called resonances

Resonance
- A resonant frequency is a natural frequency of vibration that is determined by the physical parameters of the object such as shape
- this can be used to see vocal tract shape in the signal by extracting frequency information

Fricatives
- These can be categorized by turbelent airflow so this would a small constriction and a hiss so for example like [s] and [z]
Unvoiced Fricatives
- we can look at these like with an aperiodic wave (ie a wave with no pattern where as a periodic wave has a pattern)
- this aperiodic structure is seen in frequency as it will essentially just have one big peak
- it should sound like white noise to us
Vowels
- length of each cycle is the period
- number of cycles is the frequency measured in hertz 
- you can calculate it like any other equation as f = 1/t
- The spectrum for this slice shows more structure: distinct peaks, corresponding to the dark bits in the spectrogram.
Oral Stops
- recognizable by the closure and burst in the waveform and spectogram
- they can be an aspiration so it looks like a fricative between the burst and the next vowel
- Oral stops (aka plosives) at the start of a word can be distinguished by resonant frequency (formant) transitions into the following vowel
Nasal
- characterized by a decrease in amplitude across the specturm 
- Much less energy in the higher frequencies for this [n] when followed by voiceless stop
Approximants
- look like vowels with lower energy and changing in formant trajectory

So when diving deeper, each vowel and such and such can be distinguished
- we can use formants which are their overtones to help with this
- Resonances are ordered by relative frequency
- 1st resonance = 1st formant (F1) etc etc
- These formants have different properties
	- Formant 1 aligns with tongue height so higher vowels would have lower formants and low vowels have high f1
	- Formant 2 aligns with frontness so front vowels have high f2 and back vowels have low f2
	- Plotting formant values on a graph like this then matches where we expect these vowels to be in our mouths!
	- it is important to realize all front vowels are not all equal in frontness
	- Descriptions like front/back, high/low may actually be more indicative of acoustic differences as captured by formant
	- Vowel formant plots generally show a lot of within speaker and between speaker variation
	- There is a lot of variation between speakers within and between dialects so → We need statistical methods!
	- Formant transitions into and out of vowels can be good indicators of place of articulation, e.g. for oral stops
